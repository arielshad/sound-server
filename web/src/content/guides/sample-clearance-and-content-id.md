## Two copyrights in every record

A released song carries two separate rights. The **sound recording** (the master) usually belongs to the label or the artist who paid for it. The **composition** (melody, lyrics, chords) belongs to the songwriters and their publishers. Sampling a record uses both, so clearing a sample means agreeing terms with both owners: often an upfront fee, a share of royalties, or both. Services such as Tracklib sell pre-cleared samples from original recordings for exactly this reason; Sound Server marks those `clearance-required`.

This is a practical overview, not legal advice. Laws differ between countries, and anything with real money attached deserves a lawyer.

## Myths worth dropping

- **"Under a few seconds is fine."** There is no universal safe length. Short samples have been found infringing; courts disagree on where the line is.
- **"It's fine if I credit them."** A credit is not permission. It only satisfies licences, such as CC BY, that ask for one.
- **"It's fine if I don't make money."** Uploading uncleared material can still be infringement, and platforms remove it either way.
- **"I bought the record."** Owning a copy gives you no right to reuse the recording.

## Licensed samples don't need clearing

Sounds published under CC0, CC BY or a royalty-free licence come with permission already attached, within that licence's terms. That's the point of using them: nothing to negotiate, only conditions to follow, such as giving credit. Sound Server's verdicts summarise those conditions, and the Commercial-safe switch keeps only sounds you can release.

## Content ID and fingerprinting

YouTube's Content ID, and similar systems used by distributors and other platforms, compare new uploads with reference audio supplied by rights holders. A match can block a video, track it or redirect its revenue. The systems match audio, not licences, so a free sample can produce a claim when someone else has registered a track containing it.

If you get a claim on a sound you're allowed to use:

1. Find the sample's licence and source URL. If you kept the credit line or ATTRIBUTION.md, both are in it.
2. Dispute the claim on the platform, quoting the licence and linking to the sample's page.
3. If the claimant registered a raw sample they don't exclusively own, the dispute usually ends with the claim released.

Less common sounds and heavy processing make false matches less likely, and keeping your paperwork makes disputes quick.
